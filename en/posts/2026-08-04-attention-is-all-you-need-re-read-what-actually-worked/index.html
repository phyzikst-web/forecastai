Attention Is All You Need, Re-read: What Actually Worked

Data Science
Artificial Intelligence
LLM
Transformer
Paper Review

The 2017 Transformer paper is remembered for one slogan, but its lasting contribution is more specific and more contested than “attention is all you need.” This review separates the paper’s genuine mechanism — a constant-path, fully parallel interaction layer — from the supporting machinery that later work showed was doing much of the load-bearing, with runnable NumPy code that reproduces the scaling argument, the tensor bookkeeping, and the cost model.

The bottleneck the paper set out to remove

By 2017 the dominant sequence models were recurrent. An encoder–decoder LSTM with attention on top (Sutskever et al. 2014; Bahdanau et al. 2015) held the state of the art in machine translation, and the attention module of Bahdanau et al. (2015) had already shown that letting the decoder look back at all encoder states, rather than a single compressed vector, removed a real information bottleneck. The problem was not accuracy but the recurrence itself: to compute the hidden state at position \(t\) you must first compute the state at \(t-1\). That sequential dependency makes training hard to parallelize across the time axis and stretches the path a gradient must travel between two distant positions to a length that grows with their separation.

Vaswani et al. (2017) asked what happens if you keep the attention and throw away the recurrence. The answer — the Transformer — replaced recurrent and convolutional layers with stacked self-attention. That is the move the title advertises. But the paper’s slogan has aged into a slight misreading: the architecture is not attention alone. It is attention wrapped in residual connections, layer normalization, a position-wise feed-forward network, and a fixed positional signal, and the ablations in the paper plus a decade of follow-up show that several of those wrappers are not optional decoration. This review works through what the mechanism is, what the paper’s own experiments actually establish, and where the popular summary overstates the case.

One mechanism, applied everywhere

Self-attention computes, for every position, a weighted average of the representations at all positions, where the weights come from a compatibility score between that position’s query and every other position’s key. Given packed matrices \(Q, K, V\) with shapes \((n, d_k), (n, d_k), (n, d_v)\) the paper’s scaled dot-product attention is

\[\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V .\]

The softmax runs over the key axis, so each output row is a convex combination of value vectors. Nothing here is recurrent: every output position is computed from the same \(Q, K, V\) in one matrix multiply, which is exactly the property that lets a GPU fill the whole \((n, n)\) score matrix in parallel. The same operation is reused in three roles — encoder self-attention, decoder masked self-attention, and encoder–decoder cross-attention — differing only in where \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) come from and whether a causal mask blanks out future keys.

Two design choices turn this bare operation into something trainable. The first is the \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) scale. The second is splitting the computation into multiple heads. Both are easy to state and easy to underestimate, so it is worth reproducing what each one does.

Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)

If the entries of \(Q\) and \(K\) are independent with unit variance, a single dot product \(q^{\top}k\) sums \(d_k\) such products and therefore has variance \(d_k\). As \(d_k\) grows the logits fed to the softmax spread out, and softmax with large-magnitude inputs saturates: almost all the mass collapses onto a single key and the gradient through the other logits vanishes. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) restores unit-scale logits regardless of head width. The following code makes the effect measurable by comparing the variance of the raw and scaled scores and the entropy of the resulting attention distribution over 50 keys.

import numpy as np

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    z = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def entropy(scores):
    p = softmax(scores)
    return float(-(p * np.log(p + 1e-12)).sum())

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for d_k in [16, 64, 256]:
    Q = rng.standard_normal((1, d_k))
    K = rng.standard_normal((50, d_k))
    raw = (Q @ K.T)[0]
    scaled = raw / np.sqrt(d_k)
    print(f"d_k={d_k:>3} | var(raw)={raw.var():7.2f} | var(scaled)={scaled.var():5.3f} "
          f"| entropy raw={entropy(raw):5.3f} | entropy scaled={entropy(scaled):5.3f} "
          f"| max weight raw={softmax(raw).max():.3f} scaled={softmax(scaled).max():.3f}")

The raw variance climbs with \(d_k\) exactly as predicted, and at \(d_k = 256\) the unscaled softmax has collapsed entirely: entropy is numerically zero and one key takes all the weight. The scaled version keeps entropy near the healthy middle of its range (uniform over 50 keys would be \(\log 50 \approx 3.91\)). Sweeping \(d_k\) across a wider range and averaging over many random draws shows the collapse is systematic, not an artifact of one seed.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

dks = np.array([4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512])
ent_raw, ent_scaled = [], []
for d_k in dks:
    er, es = [], []
    for _ in range(200):
        Q = rng.standard_normal((1, d_k)); K = rng.standard_normal((50, d_k))
        raw = (Q @ K.T)[0]
        er.append(entropy(raw)); es.append(entropy(raw / np.sqrt(d_k)))
    ent_raw.append(np.mean(er)); ent_scaled.append(np.mean(es))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 3.6))
ax.plot(dks, ent_raw, "o-", label="unscaled  QK^T")
ax.plot(dks, ent_scaled, "s-", label=r"scaled  QK^T / $\sqrt{d_k}$")
ax.axhline(np.log(50), ls="--", color="gray", lw=0.8, label="uniform (log 50)")
ax.set_xscale("log", base=2); ax.set_xlabel("head dimension $d_k$")
ax.set_ylabel("mean softmax entropy (nats)")
ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

The unscaled curve decays toward zero as heads widen — the network would start training with attention distributions already saturated, and the paper notes this is why the scale factor exists. Read the figure as a warning about initialization geometry: without the \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) term, the very first forward pass on a wide head produces near-one-hot attention, and the gradient signal that would let the model learn where to attend is gone before training begins.

Multi-head attention, with the dimensions tracked

A single attention head averages values, and an average cannot simultaneously capture “the subject three tokens back” and “the nearest punctuation.” Multi-head attention runs \(h\) attention operations in parallel on linear projections of the input into \(d_k = d_\text{model}/h\)-dimensional subspaces, then concatenates and projects the results. The projections give each head its own view of what counts as a good query–key match. The bookkeeping is where implementations go wrong, so the code prints the shape at every step for \(d_\text{model}=64\), \(h=8\), sequence length 10.

seq_len, d_model, h = 10, 64, 8
d_k = d_model // h
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X  = rng.standard_normal((seq_len, d_model))
Wq = rng.standard_normal((d_model, d_model)) / np.sqrt(d_model)
Wk = rng.standard_normal((d_model, d_model)) / np.sqrt(d_model)
Wv = rng.standard_normal((d_model, d_model)) / np.sqrt(d_model)
Wo = rng.standard_normal((d_model, d_model)) / np.sqrt(d_model)

def split_heads(M):                       # (seq, d_model) -> (h, seq, d_k)
    return M.reshape(seq_len, h, d_k).transpose(1, 0, 2)

Q, K, V   = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv
Qh, Kh, Vh = split_heads(Q), split_heads(K), split_heads(V)
scores = Qh @ Kh.transpose(0, 2, 1) / np.sqrt(d_k)     # (h, seq, seq)
attn   = softmax(scores, axis=-1)
ctx    = attn @ Vh                                      # (h, seq, d_k)
ctx_merged = ctx.transpose(1, 0, 2).reshape(seq_len, d_model)
out = ctx_merged @ Wo

print(f"X            {X.shape}")
print(f"Q = K = V    {Q.shape}")
print(f"per-head Qh  {Qh.shape}  (h={h}, d_k={d_k})")
print(f"scores       {scores.shape}  (h, seq, seq)")
print(f"attn rowsum  {np.round(attn[0].sum(1)[:3], 6)}  (softmax normalized)")
print(f"context      {ctx.shape}  ->  merged {ctx_merged.shape}")
print(f"output       {out.shape}")

The total parameter and compute budget is nearly identical to a single head of full width, because each head operates in a \(d_k = 8\) subspace; the split buys representational diversity almost for free. What multi-head attention does not buy is interpretability, a point worth stating because it is routinely overclaimed. A single head’s weight matrix is a valid probability distribution over keys, but that does not make it an explanation of the model’s decision.

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.6, 3.8))
im = ax.imshow(attn[0], cmap="viridis", vmin=0)
ax.set_xlabel("key position"); ax.set_ylabel("query position")
ax.set_title("Attention weights (head 0)")
plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax, fraction=0.046)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Each row of the heatmap sums to one, so it reads like an explanation of “what this token looked at.” But the value vectors it averages, and the seven other heads and the feed-forward network downstream, all reshape that signal before it reaches the output. Treating one head’s weights as the model’s reasoning is the attention-map analogue of reading tea leaves; the weights are real, their causal role in the prediction is not established by the picture.

What the ablations actually showed

The paper’s contribution is not just the architecture but Table 3, where the authors vary one component at a time on English–German translation. Read critically, that table is where the “attention is all you need” reading gets complicated. Reducing the number of heads to one drops BLEU noticeably, and using too many heads also hurts — so the multiplicity of heads matters, not attention per se. Shrinking the key dimension \(d_k\) degrades quality, consistent with the compatibility function being the load-bearing part. Enlarging the model and adding dropout both help in the expected directions. Crucially, replacing the learned components with the sinusoidal positional encoding produced nearly identical results to a learned positional embedding, which is the evidence behind the common claim that the specific positional scheme is not what makes the model work.

Two cautions apply to how this table is usually cited. First, every number is a single run on one language pair with one tokenizer and one training budget; the paper does not report variance across seeds, so small BLEU gaps between configurations should not be read as reliable rankings. Second, the ablations vary components that were already inside the Transformer. They tell you the relative importance of heads, key width, and positional scheme, but they do not isolate the residual connections or layer normalization, because no row of the table removes those. For the components the paper did not ablate, the evidence comes from later work, and it is not favorable to the “attention alone” reading.

Cost and path length: the real reason it scaled

The section of the paper that has aged best is the complexity comparison. A self-attention layer costs \(O(n^2 \cdot d)\) operations and connects any two positions through a path of length \(O(1)\); a recurrent layer costs \(O(n \cdot d^2)\) and has a maximum path length of \(O(n)\). The code below evaluates both for \(d = 512\) across sequence lengths.

d = 512
ns = np.array([32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096])
self_ops = ns**2 * d
rec_ops  = ns * d * d

The crossover at \(n = d = 512\) is the detail most summaries omit. Self-attention is cheaper per layer only while the sequence is shorter than the model width; past that point its quadratic cost overtakes the recurrent layer, which is why long-context Transformers spawned an entire literature on sub-quadratic attention. The advantage that does not degrade is the path length: it is \(1\) for self-attention at every sequence length, versus \(n\) for recurrence.

The left panel shows the crossover; the right panel shows the property that made the architecture trainable at depth. A constant-length path between any two positions means the gradient signal linking distant tokens does not have to survive \(n\) successive Jacobian products, which is the failure mode that made long-range learning in RNNs fragile. This — parallelism plus short gradient paths — is the mechanism that actually scaled, more than any claim about attention being a uniquely expressive operation.

What actually worked

Pulling the threads together, the durable contribution of the paper is narrower and more structural than its title. Self-attention supplied a fully parallel, constant-path interaction layer, and that removed the two properties of recurrence that capped model size and depth. But the components the paper under-emphasized turn out to be doing much of the work. Residual connections, imported from He et al. (2016), are what let the encoder and decoder stack to depth without the signal degrading; remove them and a deep Transformer does not train. Layer normalization (Ba et al. 2016) stabilizes the scale of activations between sublayers. And the position of that normalization — the paper placed it after each sublayer (Post-LN), whereas later work showed that placing it before (Pre-LN) removes the need for the learning-rate warmup the original recipe required and makes deep Transformers train more reliably (Xiong et al. 2020). That the field quietly switched to Pre-LN is direct evidence that the original arrangement was not optimal, and that the architecture is sensitive to details the slogan ignores.

So the honest reading is that attention was necessary but not sufficient. The paper’s real achievement was assembling attention, residual connections, normalization, and a position-wise feed-forward network into a block that trains stably when stacked and parallelizes across the sequence. Ablating attention would of course break it; but so would ablating the residuals, and the paper’s own experiments only tested the former.

Limits and what the paper did not settle

Three limitations bound how far the 2017 results generalize. The quadratic cost demonstrated above is a real ceiling: the architecture as published does not scale to very long sequences without modification, and the crossover at \(n = d\) shows the problem starts earlier than the “linear enough in practice” framing suggests. The evaluation was narrow — two translation tasks and an English constituency-parsing check — so the paper establishes that the Transformer matches or beats recurrent baselines on those tasks, not the general-purpose dominance the architecture later achieved, which took the scale-up in Devlin et al. (2019) and successors to demonstrate. And the interpretability of attention weights, sometimes advertised as a side benefit, is not supported by the paper’s evidence and has been contested since; the weights are a real intermediate quantity, but their causal relation to the output is not shown by inspecting them.

Conclusion

Re-read a decade later, “attention is all you need” is best treated as a provocation rather than a finding. The finding is that a specific block — scaled dot-product multi-head attention, wrapped in residual connections, layer normalization, and a feed-forward network — gives a sequence model both full parallelism and a constant-length gradient path, and that this combination scales where recurrence did not. The \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) scale and the multi-head split are the parts of attention that had to be right, and the code here shows why. But the residual and normalization machinery, which the paper mentions almost in passing and never ablates, is doing enough of the load-bearing that later work reorganized it (Pre-LN) for stability. For anyone using or teaching the Transformer, the useful lesson is not the slogan but the decomposition: know which piece supplies parallelism, which supplies trainable depth, and which supplies the positional signal, because the next architecture you read will keep some of them and replace others.
